Agentifying a Lengthy Static Form for a Local Agency

An agentic solution built for a real-world media intake process, and why the intelligence behind the automation matters as much as the technology itself.

Share

I've been building business automations for eight years, and if there's one thing I've learned, it's that some of the biggest opportunities aren't hiding inside complicated business processes.

Sometimes they're sitting right in front of us.

A form. A spreadsheet. A repetitive task that everyone has accepted as part of doing business.

And nobody questions it because, well, that's how it's always been done.

Until somebody does.

The Opportunity Was Right in Front of Me

I've had a relationship with Buzz Media Group in Jacksonville for several years. I've appeared on their business programming numerous times, discussing topics ranging from customer engagement and retention to Ai-powered automation.

Recently, I was invited back for another segment of Advertising Champions.

As part of preparing for these appearances, guests complete a business profile questionnaire.

I've filled out that questionnaire more times than I care to count.

And that got me thinking.

Why am I repeatedly entering information into a traditional form when the very technology I work with every day could make the experience a more intelligent conversational, and efficient for the editorial team at the studio?

That was the opportunity.

Not to build another chatbot.

To rethink the process.

From a Static Form to a Meaningful Conversation

Traditional forms have their place. Back in 1999..They're predictable, rigid, inexpensive, and impersonal. If you miss a question or if an answer doesn't fit a specific format, the form does not get accepted

A smart agent can approach the same assignment differently.

Instead of presenting a series of fields, it engages the person in a conversation. It asks a question, interprets the response, remembers what was said, and moves forward.

If someone provides information out of order, that's fine. If they want to correct an answer, the agent can accommodate it.

If they've already answered a question naturally during the conversation, there's no reason to ask it again.

Building the Proof of Concept

I decided to build a functional prototype using MCP Factor, an agentic platform that allows Ai operators and teams build agents to interact with business systems and perform actions beyond simply generating responses.

The assignment was relatively straightforward.

Create an Ai-powered business profile intake experience that could:

  • Guide a guest through a natural conversation
  • Understand and organize their business information
  • Accommodate corrections and conversational responses
  • Save structured information into the contact database
  • Transfer the collected data into Google Sheets realtime
  • Provide a clean, professional interface for the guest
  • Be compliant and ask for consent in a non-robotic manner

The prototype included a branded landing page, a conversational Ai agent, structured data collection, and integrations with the systems receiving the user's data.

When the Ai Has to Think, Not Just Follow Instructions

One of the most revealing moments came during testing.

The agent was asking questions, collecting answers, and progressing through the intake.

Great.

But collecting information isn't the same as understanding it.

What happens when the guest answers two questions at once or when they get distracted, busy and abort the mission?

This is very common human behavior.

During testing, I deliberately interacted with the agent conversationally rather than following a neat, predictable script.

I corrected answers. I provided additional context. I responded in ways that didn't necessarily fit the suggested options and skipped some questions.

The agent was able to handle all of it.

The Moment the Conversation Became Useful Business Data

A conversation by itself doesn't accomplish much operationally if the information stays trapped inside a chat window.

This is where the agentic component becomes significant.

As the guest provides information, the system captures structured data that can be used by other business tools.

In our tests, we successfully transferred information into the platform's contact database while also being backed realtime in Google Sheets.

Think about the implications.

Information collected during a natural conversation can become an organized business record without someone manually copying answers from a questionnaire.

That record can potentially support production preparation, guest coordination, future communication, or other workflows.

The same principle extends far beyond a media company.

A real estate agency collecting buyer requirements.

A medical practice handling nonclinical intake information.

A professional services firm qualifying prospective clients.

An event organizer registering attendees.

Different industries, similar operational friction/solution.

The technology changes the experience of collecting information and creates opportunities to use that information more effectively afterward.

Of course, a successful test isn't the same as a production deployment. Our prototype still required complete end-to-end validation before anyone should depend on it operationally.

But the core capability was demonstrated.

And that was the purpose.

The Human Element Still Matters

Here's where my experience as an Automation Specialist comes into play.

Ai can suggest architectures, write instructions, connect components, and troubleshoot configurations.

It's becoming remarkably capable at all of those things.

But it doesn't automatically understand why a particular business process exists, what actually makes that process frustrating, or whether a proposed improvement is worth implementing.

That judgment matters.

For example, we could have built a much more elaborate system.

Multiple flows, a fancy mobile wallet guest credential, deploy it via voice instead of just text, pipeline automations, I can go on..

Would that have made the solution better?

Not necessarily.

It could have made it more expensive to maintain, more complicated to operate, and too overkill for a prototype demo.

The assignment wasn't to demonstrate how much technology we could deploy.

It was to demonstrate how little unnecessary friction a well-designed solution could leave behind.

And that requires more than knowing which buttons to press.

It requires understanding the business objective, recognizing the constraints, and making deliberate architectural decisions.

A Different Way to Demonstrate Value

One thing I've learned throughout my career is that describing what Ai can do rarely has the same impact as showing someone a working example.

Anybody can walk into a business and say they offer Ai automation.

The real question is: what have you identified in that business that could genuinely benefit from it?

In this case, I didn't start with a list of Ai products or features. I started with a process I had personally experienced.

I recognized the friction.

I considered how the process could work differently, then I built a proof of concept that demonstrated the alternative.

There's a substantial difference between selling someone technology and showing them an opportunity they hadn't considered.

Or maybe they did, but the Specialist hadn't walked in with it.

The latter creates a much more interesting business conversation.

Beyond the Prototype

The Buzz Media demonstration wasn't intended to replace an existing production system overnight.

It was built to explore a possibility and demonstrate a working alternative.

There are still practical questions any organization would need to answer before adopting something like this.

How should the data be validated? Who needs access to it? How should returning guests be recognized? What happens when information is incomplete? How does the new process fit into the existing operation?

Those are implementation questions, not reasons to avoid innovation.

And they're precisely why experienced human oversight remains important.

An agentic solution shouldn't be judged by how impressive its interface looks or how many integrations it contains.

It should be judged by whether it makes a business process measurably better.

Does it save time?

Does it reduce repetitive work?

Does it improve the experience?

Does it produce information the organization can actually use?

Those are the questions worth asking.

The Bigger Picture

We're entering a period where businesses can do far more than automate repetitive clicks and predefined sequences.

They can deploy systems that interpret information, make contextual decisions, interact with other software, and help move business processes forward.

But there's a catch.

Giving Ai the ability to act doesn't automatically give it the judgment to act appropriately.

The quality of the solution still depends heavily on the person identifying the problem, designing the process, establishing the boundaries, and validating the results.

That's what this demonstration reinforced for me.

The future of business automation isn't about replacing every form with a chatbot or attaching Ai to every available process.

It's about recognizing when intelligence adds meaningful value and building the right solution around that opportunity.

Sometimes that opportunity starts with something as ordinary as a business profile questionnaire.

And sometimes all it takes to see it is asking a different question:

Why are we still doing it this way?
